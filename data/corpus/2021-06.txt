Noise is usually treated as an annoyance rather than a health problem, but medical researchers now classify it as a serious risk. Long-term exposure to traffic noise has been linked to high blood pressure, disturbed sleep and, in children, slower progress in reading. The mechanism is straightforward: the body reacts to sudden sound as if it were a threat, and repeated activation of this response eventually damages the cardiovascular system. What makes the problem difficult to solve is that people adapt to noise without becoming immune to it. Residents who insist that they no longer notice the traffic often show the same physical signs of stress as those who complain. City authorities have begun to measure sound levels in the same way they measure air quality, and a few of them have introduced quiet zones where vehicles are restricted. Early results indicate that even a modest reduction in noise produces measurable health benefits within a few years.

@body
The rapid expansion of food delivery has created a large workforce whose conditions differ sharply from those of traditional employees. Riders are usually classified as independent contractors, which means they are not covered by labour regulations and must supply their own equipment. They value the flexibility: many of them are students or parents who could not accept a fixed schedule. Yet the same flexibility transfers every risk onto the worker. If a rider is injured, or if the restaurant is slow, the loss falls on them alone. Companies point out that the system allows anyone to start earning within a day, and that attempts to regulate the industry have reduced the number of available jobs. Researchers who interviewed riders found a more complicated picture: most of them accepted the arrangement for a period, but very few expected to remain in it for years, and almost none had any form of insurance.

## 仔细阅读 | Passage One
@topic 仔细阅读：网络谣言为什么传播得比真相快
@question 46. What does the passage mainly discuss?
@choice A) The technology behind social networks.
@choice B) Why false information spreads faster than the truth.
@choice C) How to punish people who spread rumours.
@choice D) The history of journalism.
@question 47. What did the study of millions of messages find?
@choice A) False stories are usually longer than true ones.
@choice B) Falsehoods travel further and faster because they are more novel.
@choice C) People share only information they believe.
@choice D) Rumours disappear within a day.
@body
A study that examined millions of messages shared online reached an uncomfortable conclusion: false information travels faster, further and deeper than accurate information. The explanation is not that people are foolish. It is that a rumour is usually more surprising than the truth, and surprise is what makes something worth passing on. A story about an ordinary event produces no desire to share it, while a story that seems incredible demands an audience. Researchers measured this by comparing how novel each message was, and found that falsehoods were consistently rated as more unexpected. There is a second factor. People share material that expresses emotion, and rumours tend to be written in emotional language, whereas corrections are sober and careful, which makes them far less attractive. Platforms have tried various responses, from warnings to removing content, but the most effective measure appears to be slower: teaching users to check the source before sharing. Studies suggest that even a brief pause, prompted by a simple question, reduces the number of false stories people forward. The problem cannot be solved by deleting rumours alone, because the demand for surprising stories remains.

## 仔细阅读 | Passage Two
@topic 仔细阅读：选择专业时应该看兴趣还是就业前景
@question 50. What is the author's conclusion?
@choice A) Students must choose the subject with the highest salary.
@choice B) Interest and employment prospects should both be weighed, with interest given more weight.
@choice C) Universities should decide for their students.
@choice D) Employment data is useless.
@body
Every year, families argue about the same question: should a student choose the subject they love, or the one that leads to a secure job? The debate is usually presented as a choice between idealism and realism, but the evidence does not support such a sharp distinction. Graduates who selected a subject mainly because of its employment prospects are not necessarily happier in their work, and a substantial proportion of them leave the field within five years. Interest, meanwhile, has a practical value that is easy to overlook: students who are genuinely curious study harder, absorb material faster, and are more willing to persist when a course becomes difficult. That said, it would be dishonest to ignore the market. A sensible approach is to treat employment data as information rather than as an instruction, and to ask how a subject can be combined with skills that employers value, such as writing clearly, analysing data or speaking another language. The students who do best are rarely those who followed a formula. They are the ones who found something they were willing to work at, and then made sure that other people could see the value of it.

## 翻译 | 筷子与餐桌礼仪
@topic 汉译英：筷子的使用与文化含义（参考译文）
@body
Chopsticks have been used in China for at least three thousand years, and they reflect a practical attitude towards food: dishes are cut into small pieces before they are served, so a knife at the table is unnecessary. There are also rules about how chopsticks should be used. For example, they should not be stood upright in a bowl of rice, because this resembles the incense burned for the dead, and it is considered unlucky. Parents teach these rules to their children from an early age, and visitors who observe them are usually praised for their manners.

## 写作 | 范文
@topic 写作：Is cash still necessary?（参考范文）
@body
Electronic payment has become so common that some people predict the end of cash. It is certainly convenient: a transaction takes seconds, there is no risk of receiving the wrong change, and every purchase is recorded automatically. However, I do not think cash should disappear completely. Many elderly people still find digital payment confusing, and they would be excluded from ordinary shopping if notes and coins were abolished. There is also the question of privacy, since electronic records reveal a great deal about a person's habits. For these reasons, cash should remain an option rather than a rarity, and shops that accept only cards or phones should be required to say so at the entrance.
